    Practice guide

    Reading music is pattern recognition. You don't name every note: you find the first note, then read the shape (steps, skips, chords) and the rhythm. These are the pieces you need for the exercises in this app.

    1. Where the notes are on the keyboard

    The black keys come in groups of two and three. C is the white key just left of each group of two. The C nearest the middle of the piano is middle C (C4), shown in pink; the number is the octave, counting up from the bottom.

    2. The two staves

    Piano music uses two staves joined by a brace. The treble clef (top, usually right hand) and the bass clef (bottom, usually left hand). Middle C sits on a short extra line between them.

    Treble clef

    Lines: E G B D F, "Every Good Boy Does Fine". Spaces: F A C E.

    Bass clef

    Lines: G B D F A, "Good Boys Do Fine Always". Spaces: A C E G, "All Cows Eat Grass".

    Ledger lines are short extra lines above or below a staff. Count them like normal lines: one ledger line below the treble staff is middle C, one above is A5.

    3. Note lengths and counting

    The click of the metronome is the beat. In 4/4 there are four beats per bar. Shorter notes split the beat:

    Per click

    • Quarter note: 1 note
    • 8th notes (one beam): 2 notes, count "1-and"
    • Triplets (a 3 over the group): 3 notes, "1-trip-let"
    • 16th notes (two beams): 4 notes, "1-e-and-a"
    • Sextuplets: 6 notes, two triplets in one click

    Dots and ties

    • A dot adds half the note's length: a dotted quarter lasts 1½ beats.
    • A tie joins two notes of the same pitch: play once and hold for both.
    • A slur is the same curve over different notes: play them legato (smoothly connected).

    Rests: silence of the same lengths

    4. Key signatures and accidentals

    The sharps or flats at the start of each line are the key signature: they apply to that note in every octave for the whole piece. An accidental next to a note (♯ sharp, ♭ flat, ♮ natural) changes it until the end of that bar only. This is D minor: one flat (B♭), with C♯ added as an accidental.

    5. Fingering, articulation and voices

    • Finger numbers above or below notes: thumb is 1, little finger 5, in both hands.
    • Dots under or over notes mean staccato: short and detached.
    • Two voices on one staff: stems up are one voice, stems down another. In "Hold and move", the stems-down whole note is held while the stems-up notes move.

    6. How to read an exercise before you play

    1. Find the key (key signature) and the first note of each hand.
    2. Find the pattern: does it go up by step (scale), by skip (chord), or repeat? Almost every exercise is one pattern moved around.
    3. Find the highest and lowest notes, and where the thumb passes (the finger numbers).
    4. Check the rhythm: how many notes per click.
    5. While playing, keep your eyes one beat ahead of your hands. Don't look down unless you're jumping.

    Speed comes from accuracy repeated, not from trying to be fast. The rule: never practise mistakes at speed. Every clean slow repetition makes the next tempo possible.

    What the numbers mean

    BPM is clicks per minute; each click is a quarter-note beat. At 60 BPM there is one click per second, so 16ths are 4 notes per second. Concert speed for many 16th-note passages is 112–132 BPM (7–9 notes per second); start well below that.

    The method

    1. Notes first, no clockUse Wait for me. The music only moves when you play the right notes, so you learn notes and fingering with no time pressure. Hands separately, then together.
    2. Start slow on the clickSwitch to Test at tempo at about 60% of the suggested tempo. It should feel almost too easy. Count aloud during the count-in.
    3. Two clean runs, then +4A clean run means the report says Clean. After two clean runs in a row, press Faster (+4 BPM).
    4. Two misses, then −8If you fail twice at the same tempo, go 8 BPM slower, get two clean runs, and climb again. Bouncing around the edge is how the ceiling rises.
    5. Fix the weak spot, not the wholeThe report names your least steady bar. Loop just that bar plus the first note of the next, in Wait for me, then at tempo.
    6. Finish musicallyEnd each exercise with one run at a comfortable tempo, playing it as music: even, shaped, relaxed.

    Tricks that speed things up

    Dotted rhythms

    Play a 16th-note run as long-short, long-short, then short-long. Each pair of fingers learns to move fast in turn. Then play it even again: it will feel easier.

    Bursts

    Play one group of 4 notes fast and stop on the next beat note. Then 2 groups, then 4. Your hand learns the movement at full speed while you stay in control.

    Accents

    Accent every 3rd note in a 16th run, then every 4th. Moving the accent stops one finger always being weak.

    Short sessions

    10–15 minutes of technique, then repertoire. Two short sessions beat one long one, and sleep consolidates what you practised.

    Reading the report

    • Timing spread (±ms): how even you are. Below the target is clean. Professional evenness in scales is around ±10–15 ms.
    • Average timing: negative means rushing, positive dragging. Most people rush when nervous.
    • Hands together: how far apart notes meant to be together land. Under 15 ms sounds as one.
    • Loudness ±%: unevenness in volume. Look at the lower panel of the chart for spikes on thumb crossings.

    Fast, even playing comes from the arm and the hand working together with as little tension as possible. Fingers alone are small and tire quickly; let the arm put them in place.

    Set-up

    Sitting

    • Forearms level with the keys, or slightly higher.
    • Sit on the front half of the bench, close enough that elbows are a little in front of your body.
    • Feet flat; lean very slightly forward.

    Hand shape

    • A natural curve, like holding a small ball.
    • Play on the fingertips; the thumb on the side of its tip.
    • Wrist level with the hand, not dropped, not high.
    • Shoulders down. Check them at every rest.

    The movements

    Thumb under (scales)

    Move the thumb under the palm while fingers 2–3 play, so it arrives over its key early. The elbow drifts sideways with the hand; the wrist stays straight. Going down, 3 or 4 crosses over the thumb with a small lift and pivot.

    Lateral glide (arpeggios)

    The forearm carries the hand along the keyboard at a steady speed; fingers don't stretch. Turn the wrist slightly in the direction of travel just before the thumb passes.

    Rotation (broken octaves, Alberti)

    Rock the forearm like turning a doorknob between thumb and fifth finger. Fingers stay passive and close to the keys.

    Wrist (octaves, staccato)

    Lock the hand in the octave shape (1–5, or 1–4 on black keys) and play with a small down-up bounce of the wrist. Stay near the fallboard on black keys. Rest at every pause.

    Leaps

    Look at the target, not your hand. Move early and fast in a low arc, shape the chord in the air, land gently on the key surface and then play. The time is spent in the air, not on arrival.

    Hand crossings

    The crossing hand goes over, not under: lift it higher and move from the shoulder. The hand underneath stays low and keeps its rhythm.

    Independence (two voices)

    A held note needs only enough weight to keep the key down. Moving fingers play from the knuckle with the hand loose. If the hand tightens, slow down.

    Voicing

    Lean the arm's weight towards the melody finger, usually 4–5: a firm fingertip there, the other fingers barely touching their keys.

    Batteries and repeated notes

    Hands close together, small light wrist movements like drumming. For repeated notes, pull fingers 4-3-2-1 towards you off the key with a shallow stroke.

    Tension check

    After each run, drop your hands in your lap and shake them out. Burning in the forearm, a tight thumb or raised shoulders mean stop, rest and slow down. Pain is never part of practice.
